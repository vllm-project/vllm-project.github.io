On AgentX, an eight-GPU AMD Instinct MI355X system serving Kimi K3 with vLLM delivers **24.4% more total tokens per second per GPU** than NVIDIA HGX B300 at 50 tokens/s/user P90 interactivity. Under the benchmark’s ownership-cost model, it delivers **87.4% more tokens per dollar**.

In this post, we’ll unpack the engineering behind Kimi K3 on AMD Instinct MI355X, connecting vLLM’s serving decisions to GPU kernels and the hardware underneath. We’ll explain why the optimizations worked and build the intuition to spot wasted work, identify bottlenecks and find the next optimization in your own inference stack.

Published dense matrix peaks are 10.1 PFLOP/s MXFP4 for AMD Instinct MI355X and 13.5 PFLOP/s FP4 for NVIDIA B300. Both have 288 GB HBM3E and 8 TB/s peak memory bandwidth. Reaching this serving performance reflects sustained software engineering by AMD and collaborators across the vLLM community.

## AgentX: the workload and results

The public [AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx) benchmark replays real coding-agent session traces with synthetic tokens, retaining request lengths, timing and prefix-sharing patterns. The workload offers over 96% theoretical prefix reuse with unlimited cache. Speculative runs use a fixed synthetic acceptance reference.

The advantage varies with response speed. At 150 tokens/s/user, AMD Instinct MI355X serves 1,768 total tokens/s/GPU against 2,075 for NVIDIA HGX B300, while delivering 28.4% more tokens per modeled dollar.

## Inside Kimi K3: experts, attention and recurrent state

A returning agent turn can reuse most of its prefix, but processing a short continuation still touches three kinds of data.

Kimi K3 uses two kinds of attention. Its **24 MLA layers** retain a compressed KV history that grows with the conversation. A new query still consults that history. Its **69 KDA layers** instead carry forward state matrices with fixed dimensions, updating them as new tokens arrive. [Model configuration](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3/blob/c5d1dd4c428bd1ce8b88c5044f3b6ccde9e3b721/config.json).

In MoE layers, a router selects expert networks to transform each token. Their weights are shared across requests.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/hybrid-state.svg" alt="Kimi K3 request flow: recover compatible saved state, process the new suffix through MLA or KDA attention and routed experts, then decode or verify new positions." width="900" height="670" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>MLA retains more history as the conversation grows. KDA updates a fixed-dimension state; retaining earlier checkpoints means keeping additional copies of that state.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Across turns:** cache reuse and offload (Section 1).<br>
**Within a model step:** expert projections, kernel handoffs, and MLA/KDA scheduling (Sections 2–5).<br>
**Choosing draft depth:** committed output versus complete cycle cost (Section 6).

## 1. Cache: keep conversation state available for reuse

DCP reduces duplicate history in GPU memory; CPU offload preserves reusable state outside it.

### DCP: share the history across GPUs

Under TP8, each GPU handles 12 of Kimi K3’s 96 MLA query heads but stores the entire compressed KV history. Tensor parallelism divides the heads without dividing the cache.

With [eight-way decode context parallelism (DCP8)](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-08-07-decode-context-parallelism), each GPU stores one eighth of the cached positions. During decode, the GPUs exchange queries, compute against their local history and merge their partial answers.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/dcp-history.svg" alt="TP replicates the complete MLA history on each GPU. DCP exchanges query heads, divides cached positions between GPUs, then merges their partial answers using softmax weights." width="900" height="650" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Two GPUs illustrate the same partition used by TP8/DCP8. This extra division applies to MLA history; KDA state keeps its existing partition across heads.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Each partial answer carries normalization information so the merge can weight shards correctly. [#51705](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/51705) extends the ROCm AITER DCP path to multi-token verification.

For a 142,000-token conversation, the estimated MLA payload is 24 layers × 142,000 positions × 576 FP8 bytes ≈ 1.96 GB per GPU. DCP8 reduces this to about 245 MB, before allocation overhead. Each GPU processes eight times as many heads against one eighth of the history, leaving the leading attention arithmetic roughly unchanged.

### CPU offload: keep state that no longer fits in HBM

When state no longer fits in GPU memory, the [AMD recipe](https://github.com/SemiAnalysisAI/InferenceX/blob/8adb5c5589b84c0afdccc60bab521eb1114f52b5/inferencex-e2e/benchmarks/single_node/srt-slurm-recipes/kimik3/vllm/mi355x-fp4-mtp/agentic.yaml)’s native vLLM CPU offload preserves MLA history and KDA checkpoints in host DRAM.

A cache hit needs matching resume points. If MLA history reaches token 3,072 but the latest compatible KDA checkpoint is at 2,048, the request must resume at 2,048 and recompute the remaining prefix.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/cache-resume-boundary.svg" alt="MLA history reaches token 3,072, but the latest compatible KDA checkpoint is at 2,048. The request must resume at 2,048 and recompute the remaining 1,024 prefix positions." width="900" height="310" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>A usable resume point needs compatible state across all required layers. Positions are illustrative.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Saving the attention-block tail alongside the KDA checkpoint aligns their resume positions ([#54736](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/54736)). Restoring that state trades a transfer back to the GPU for avoided prefix recomputation. Once a request resumes, its new tokens can still make several GPUs repeat the same calculation.

<span id="moe-remove-duplicate-work-across-gpus"></span>

## 2. MoE: remove duplicate work across GPUs

On one GPU, the routed-expert output projection returns the complete vector its consumer expects. In the original ROCm path, an earlier reduction and normalization gave all eight GPUs the same latent input, and each held the full weights. **The same projection therefore ran eight times.**

The output coordinates can be divided among GPUs, but every rank still needs the complete result. Kimi K3 already has an all-reduce for its shared-expert partials.

Each GPU adds only the routed outputs it owns to its shared-expert partial. The existing all-reduce assembles the complete result, with no new collective ([#51253](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/51253)). The ROCm implementation builds on the corresponding CUDA strategy.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/moe-projection-collective.svg" alt="Before: each GPU repeats the full routed projection. After: output coordinates are divided among GPUs and added before the existing shared-expert all-reduce. Both paths give every GPU the same full output." width="1200" height="620" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Two GPUs illustrate output-coordinate ownership. Both paths use one existing all-reduce and return the same full result to every GPU.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Each output coordinate receives its routed contribution exactly once. The full projection weights remain replicated.

Output throughput rose 3.05–4.91% in the PR’s test on eight AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs at TP8, across concurrency 8–128 with random 8K-input/1K-output requests and prefix caching disabled.

### Free up GPU memory to keep more cached state

Each rank now computes only its assigned projection coordinates while retaining the full weights.

Loading only the rows each rank uses frees about **3.85 GiB per GPU** across 92 MoE layers at TP8 ([#59591](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/59591)). The fused path’s calculation stays the same; the extra space can hold MLA history and KDA checkpoints.

This change landed after the image used for the headline public result. Its separate AgentX experiment found **less than 1% higher throughput at concurrency 32 and 48, but 28.9% at 70**, where the reported prefix-cache hit rate rose from 74.8% to 80.1%.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/cache-capacity.svg" alt="PR 59591: throughput gains and prefix-cache hit rates at concurrency 32, 48, 64 and 70. Gains increase with load in these separate measurements." width="720" height="460.8" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Separate <a href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/59591">#59591 measurements</a>: eight AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs, TP8/DCP8, FP8 KV, 30-minute runs, no CPU offload or speculative decoding. Points show the reported runs; repeated-run uncertainty was not reported.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The larger gain at high concurrency is consistent with cache pressure. Comparing both implementations at equal cache capacity would help isolate that effect.

## 3. Memory traffic: fuse operations and remove unnecessary copies

Within each GPU, the next place to look is the data passed between kernels.

Kimi K3’s single-token KDA path ran three compute kernels and two output copies. Convolution ran before a kernel that already fused Q/K normalization, decay and beta gates, and the recurrent update; output normalization ran afterward. The fused HIP path performs these operations in one launch and writes directly to the destination buffer ([#50654](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/50654)).

Intermediate handoffs stay on chip. Convolution history and recurrent state still go to GPU memory for the next token.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/kda-fusion-before.svg" alt="KDA decode before fusion: three compute kernels and two copies" width="1200" height="530" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/kda-fusion-after.svg" alt="KDA decode after fusion: one HIP kernel; saved state remains" width="1200" height="530" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Single-token, non-speculative KDA decode in <a href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/50654">#50654</a>. Before uses the fused Triton normalization path. Counts follow the source code; projections are outside the view. Saved-state reads and writes remain.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

AttnRes mixes earlier layer-block outputs with the running residual. Combining that mix with RMSNorm keeps the temporary mixture in registers ([#50593](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/50593)). The running residual saved for later layers retains its BF16 rounding; the mixture stays in FP32 through normalization. Tests require exact persistent updates and allow numerical tolerances for normalized output.

With AttnRes fusion, output throughput rose 14.2% on eight AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs at TP8, with 100K-input/1K-output requests and prefix caching disabled. Long-prefill latency was effectively unchanged.

### Copy inputs only for kernels that use them

The ROCm matrix-multiplication dispatcher packed inputs before choosing a kernel. When it selected the fallback, that kernel read the original input and left the copy unused. Moving packing into the specialized branches removed these copies ([#58566](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/58566)).

The profile made the waste visible: **69 unnecessary copies per decode step**, one per KDA layer. Each moved only 1,792 bytes but took about 4.8 microseconds. Moving those bytes faster would barely help; launch overhead dominated.

  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/gemm-copy-dispatch.svg" alt="Before: prepare a contiguous input before choosing the kernel, discarding it on fallback. After: copy only inside specialized branches that consume it." width="1200" height="890" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>In the PR’s TP8, concurrency-one, six-draft, 384K-context profile, the removed copies account for 0.339 ms of copy-kernel time per step.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

## 4. MLA: faster long-context decoding with AMD AITER assembly

Kimi K3 has twelve query heads per GPU at TP8. Padding to sixteen made AMD AITER’s assembly decode path available. In the reported long-context test, time per output token fell from 43.8 to 23.6 milliseconds.

The comparison used TP8 without DCP, at concurrency one. The adapter in [#50578](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/50578) copies four query heads, packs them into the sixteen-head layout and discards their outputs. DCP8 presents a different, 96-head shape.

This preserves the model's operation because each query head attends independently over cached positions. Adding another head does not change the original head's softmax. Only the query is padded in this decode path; the stored compressed KV history stays the same size.

A long conversation gives attention plenty of work, but that work needs to be spread across the GPU. In the historical code we inspected, a conservative [scheduling hint](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/7b9f2dad8920f115c1caea36e096e43c04c3da68/vllm/v1/attention/backends/mla/rocm_aiter_mla.py#L1092) restricted the Gluon path to [one workgroup per request](https://github.com/ROCm/aiter/blob/31350226161346314b3d8882c8085bd31dce6a34/aiter/ops/triton/gluon/mla_gluon.py#L937). That workgroup still processed the entire conversation. Padding made AMD AITER’s assembly path available, with a [different policy](https://github.com/ROCm/aiter/blob/31350226161346314b3d8882c8085bd31dce6a34/aiter/mla.py#L149) for dividing the history into parallel pieces and combining the results.

Query padding enables that path." width="1200" height="640" loading="lazy" decoding="async">

  </figcaption>
</figure>

| **Input / output tokens** | **Original Gluon path** | **Padded assembly path** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1K / 1K | 22.9 ms/token | 22.8 ms/token |

PR-reported mean time per output token: eight AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs, TP8, concurrency one, MXFP4 model weights, BF16 attention and ordinary single-token decode.

The short-input case barely changes; the long-input case nearly halves time per output token. These serving measurements include query preparation and output handling.

<span id="kda-parallelize-prefill-and-speculative-verification"></span>

## 5. KDA: parallelize prefill and speculative verification

MLA can split a retained history among workgroups. KDA presents a harder scheduling problem: each token depends on the preceding state. Fused prefill in [#54038](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/54038) prepares each token group’s combined transformation before its incoming state is known.

For example, “halve the state and add two,” then “halve it and add one,” combine into “take a quarter of the starting state and add two.” This rule can be computed before the starting state arrives.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/kda-group-rules.svg" alt="Combine ordered token updates into group rules, scan the summaries to find group starting states, then replay the groups in parallel while keeping tokens within each group ordered." width="900" height="585" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>

<details class="kimi-sequence" markdown="1">
<summary>Step through the KDA dependency sequence</summary>

<iframe class="vllm-embed" src="/assets/interactive_pages/kimi-k3-kda-sequence.html" title="KDA group dependency sequence" style="display: block; width: 100%; height: 740px; border: 0;" loading="lazy"></iframe>

</details>

KDA does this with matrix transformations. An ordered scan of the group summaries determines each group’s starting state; the groups then replay their tokens in parallel. Token order stays intact, but each group no longer waits for the preceding group’s replay.

Grouping adds a second walk and expands the fused chunk path from two launches to four. It helps when few sequences or heads expose too little parallel work; otherwise, the extra passes can cost more than they save.

Within each group, the recurrent walk keeps state in matrix-accumulator registers between chunks, avoiding a global-memory write and reload. [State storage diagram.](#state-between-chunks)

<span id="KDAResume"></span>Fused KDA delivered 5.3–5.6% more input throughput than Triton at 4K and 16K input lengths in a separate three-backend comparison: eight AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs, TP8, concurrency 32, prefix caching disabled and one-token outputs. This measures the complete backend. [Benchmark results](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/56036#issuecomment-5667437134).

### Use smaller tiles for speculative verification

Short verification batches need a different split. At TP8, each GPU has 12 KDA heads with 128 value columns. Splitting each head into four 32-column tiles launches only 48 workgroups on 256 compute units. [#58045](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/58045) switches to four-column tiles, raising the count to 384.

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/kda-workgroups.svg" alt="One sequence and twelve KDA heads: dividing each head into four-column rather than thirty-two-column tiles exposes 384 instead of 48 independent workgroups." width="1200" height="755" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Each tile owns different value columns and walks the same token sequence in order. Narrower tiles increase the number of independent workgroups from 48 to 384.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

In a separate graph-replay kernel test on one AMD Instinct MI355X GPU (TP8 dimensions, one sequence, four verification positions), time fell from 16.20 to 5.61 microseconds per layer (2.89×). This includes narrower tiles, single-wave workgroups, uniform-length specialization and hoisted token-invariant work. Ragged batches retain a dynamic path; tests compare outputs and recurrent state.

<span id="speculative-decoding-choose-how-many-tokens-to-draft"></span>

## 6. Speculative decoding: choose how many tokens to draft

Faster verification can change how many draft tokens are worth checking. The useful rate is committed output divided by the full draft–verify–commit cycle time.

For example, AgentX’s fixed acceptance reference gives 3.00 expected committed tokens per cycle with three drafts, and 3.36 with four, including the recovery or bonus token. That is 12% more committed output per cycle. **Four drafts improve throughput only if the complete cycle takes less than 12% longer than three drafts.**

The [public AMD recipe](https://github.com/SemiAnalysisAI/InferenceX/blob/8adb5c5589b84c0afdccc60bab521eb1114f52b5/inferencex-e2e/benchmarks/single_node/srt-slurm-recipes/kimik3/vllm/mi355x-fp4-mtp/agentic.yaml) uses three to seven drafts with DCP1 and disables speculation at the larger DCP8 concurrency settings.

## What to optimize next

Ongoing prefill/decode disaggregation work adds another place to look: worker balance and whether transfers finish before their data is needed.

For another model or workload, start with the deployed request shapes and make three checks:

**Compare ranks.** Are several GPUs computing the same result before a collective that could assemble it?

**Follow buffers.** Does each temporary tensor have a consumer on the selected path? Can the data stay on chip?

**Count independent work.** Do the actual heads, tokens and tiles produce enough workgroups to spread the load?

Measure the complete request at the response speed you need, and check both outputs and saved state. A faster kernel matters when it shortens the work holding up the next token.

## Technical note: expert routing and precision

Kimi K3 selects 16 of 896 experts per token, so a substantial server batch can still leave few rows per expert. The resulting expert tiles may contain padding.

A4W4 reduces a different part of the traffic: the integration in [#53940](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/53940) changes activations from eight bits to four, while weights remain four-bit. Total traffic also includes BF16 intermediate outputs and expert-tile padding.

## Technical note: KDA precision and on-chip memory

Each chunk still needs to resolve dependencies between its tokens. The kernel combines them into a transformation that later matrix products can apply. That creates two practical questions: which precision preserves the answer, and how much temporary data fits on chip?

A small example shows the idea. Substitute the earlier corrections into the later ones, and each answer can be expressed directly in terms of the known inputs. The resulting coefficient table has already accounted for the chain of dependencies:

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/kda-triangular.svg" alt="Three-position scalar dependency example: combine direct and indirect correction paths into a lower-triangular coefficient table so each row uses only known inputs." width="900" height="507" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>In this scalar illustration, position one's effect on position three takes two paths: directly, −1/8; through position two, (−1/4) × (−1/4) = 1/16. Together they give −1/16, the table's lower-left entry. Zeros above the diagonal reflect that later positions cannot affect earlier ones.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The real prologue computes the inverse of the 64×64 within-chunk dependency matrix. Later matrix products use this transformation to account for those dependencies.

The inverse's intermediate values can grow and cancel. Rounding them too early loses information the final result needs. The implementation uses AMD's native [**FP32 matrix instructions**](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/instruction-set-architectures/amd-instinct-cdna4-instruction-set-architecture.pdf#page=295), retaining FP32 inputs and accumulation for the sensitive inverse. Only the completed inverse is rounded to BF16 for subsequent products. [Kernel implementation](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/af1c01499b289be555c475669ba50a88e96d846e/csrc/libtorch_stable/kimi_k3/fused_kda_chunk_kernel_rocm.cu#L1639).

### Why FP32 accumulation cannot recover rounded inputs

Consider subtracting 256 from 256.5. Both inputs are exact in FP32. At this magnitude, converting 256.5 to BF16 rounds it to 256:

| **Input precision** | **Subtraction performed in FP32** | **Result** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the original FP32 inputs | 256.5 − 256 | 0.5 |
| Round the inputs to BF16 first | 256 − 256 | 0 |

FP32 subtraction cannot recover information lost before it starts. These toy values illustrate why the inverse uses native 16×16×4 FP32 matrix instructions, retaining both input and accumulation precision.

An FP4 model also relies on operations at higher precision. Their performance depends on making those numerical paths efficient.

The prologue also has a local-memory budget. AMD calls its on-chip shared scratch LDS. Q and K tiles reuse storage because they are needed at different times; the triangular input stores only its ten occupied 16×16 blocks. The workgroup requests **79.5 KiB**, which rounds to **80 KiB**, half of an AMD CDNA 4 compute unit’s [160 KiB LDS budget](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/instruction-set-architectures/amd-instinct-cdna4-instruction-set-architecture.pdf#page=14).

<figure class="kimi-figure">
  <img src="/assets/figures/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-mi355x/lds-budget.svg" alt="Within a 160 KiB LDS budget, two 80 KiB allocations fit; two 81.25 KiB allocations do not. Allocation rounds to 1,280-byte units." width="900" height="443" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>
    <p>Allocation rounds to 1,280-byte units. The illustrated extra KiB crosses a residency threshold: two allocations no longer fit. This is a capacity calculation; registers and other resources can impose additional limits.</p>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Test this threshold by reserving enough unused LDS to cross it while keeping the kernel binary fixed. If runtime barely changes, the extra resident workgroup was contributing little for that shape.
